SELTZER: A Monthly Infrastructure Review

npalladium

2026-11-16

At a previous workplace, I scheduled a monthly infrastructure review1 with the product manager, the engineering manager, and occasionally other executives. We looked for ways to cut costs and improve the infrastructure.

We started with USE, RED, and the golden signals from Google's SRE book, alongside costs. Other questions gradually joined the agenda. One day we joked that we could call the whole thing SELTZER, but never managed to fill in all the letters.

Here's my attempt to finish it. For a “backronym”, it fits those discussions suspiciously well.

  • Saturation: how full are we? Which resources are running out of headroom?
  • Errors: how often do requests fail?
  • Latency: how long does it take?
  • Traffic: how much is coming in?2

  • Zoom In: what's hiding behind the averages?3 Look at tails, individual services or tenants, and short bursts.4

    • Zzz: will this let us sleep at night? Will it wake someone unnecessarily—or fail to wake them when it should? Are we comfortable running this architecture and handling its failures?5
  • Economics: what is this costing us, including people's time? How efficiently are we using our resources? Where useful,6 calculate costs per request, use case, or customer. Sometimes infrastructure as code needs infrastructure as a Google Sheet.
  • Risks: what could go wrong, how likely and harmful would it be, and how would we recover from various DR/incident scenarios?
1

One reason for the regular reviews was the amount of experimental work and the frequent changes to which services were in production. I suspect the review is also useful when the infrastructure is fairly stable. The services may stay the same while demand, costs, and operational risks change.

2

For a while, we considered T for toil, which was also important in these reviews. Here, its time cost falls under Economics, and its operational consequences under Risks and Zzz.

3

Watermelon metrics are green on the outside, red inside: headline indicators meet their targets while users struggle. Aggregation can hide slow requests or a struggling customer; the chosen metric may also miss what users actually need.

4

A mean does not describe a workload's variability or tails, which can matter to queueing behavior. See Mor Harchol-Balter's Performance Modeling and Design of Computer Systems: Queueing Theory in Action.

5

The sleep question also covers noisy alerts and fragile deployments, even when the graphs look fine.

6

On deciding what is worth measuring: Douglas W. Hubbard, How to Measure Anything.
